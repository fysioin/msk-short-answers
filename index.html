Q1 Professional Values of Physiotherapy

Physiotherapy is a health profession that helps people restore and maintain movement and function. Like all health professions, it is guided by a set of core values and ethical principles. This question asks about the professional values that guide physiotherapy practice.

Meaning

  • Professional values are the beliefs and principles that guide a physiotherapist's behaviour.
  • They shape how a therapist treats patients, colleagues and society.
  • They are set by bodies like World Physiotherapy (WCPT).

Core Professional Values

  • Accountability — taking responsibility for one's own actions.
  • Altruism — putting the patient's interest first.
  • Compassion and caring — showing kindness and empathy.
  • Excellence — giving high-quality, evidence-based care.
  • Integrity — being honest and fair.
  • Social responsibility — serving community health needs.

Ethical Principles

  • Autonomy — respect the patient's choices and consent.
  • Beneficence — do good for the patient.
  • Non-maleficence — do no harm.
  • Justice — treat all patients equally.
  • Confidentiality — protect patient information.

Importance in Practice

  • Builds trust between patient and therapist.
  • Guides good clinical decision-making.
  • Maintains the reputation of the profession.
  • Protects patient rights and safety.

Professional values form the ethical backbone of physiotherapy and guide safe, respectful, patient-centred care.

Recent advances

  • Growing emphasis on patient-centred and culturally sensitive care.
  • New guidelines on ethics in telerehabilitation and digital practice.

References

  • World Physiotherapy. Policy statement: Ethical responsibilities of physical therapists.
  • Purtilo RB, Doherty RF. Ethical Dimensions in the Health Professions.
  • Dutton M. Dutton's Orthopaedic Examination, Evaluation and Intervention.

Q2 Designing a Research Study

Research is a careful and systematic way of finding answers to a question using evidence. In physiotherapy, good research tells us which treatments actually work for patients. This question asks how a research study is designed, that is, planned step by step before it is carried out.

Meaning and purpose

  • Research = an organised search for new facts.
  • Purpose: to answer one clear question using data.
  • It follows the scientific method.
  • Must be systematic, valid and reproducible.

Steps in designing a study

  • Choose a clear research question.
  • Review previous literature on the topic.
  • Write the aim and objectives.
  • Select the design, sample and tools.
  • Collect, analyse and report the data.
  • Question→Design→Data→Conclusion

Common study designs

  • Observational: cross-sectional, cohort, case-control.
  • Experimental: randomised controlled trial (RCT).
  • Descriptive: case study, survey.
  • Mixed designs use both types together.

Important points

  • Define the population and sample clearly.
  • Use a proper sampling method.
  • State inclusion and exclusion criteria.
  • Reduce bias and confounding.
  • Take ethical clearance and patient consent.

A well-designed study gives reliable, ethical and useful results.

Recent advances

  • Use of digital apps and wearables for data collection.
  • Growing use of evidence-based practice and systematic reviews.

References

  • Portney LG, Watkins MP. Foundations of Clinical Research.
  • Bowling A. Research Methods in Health.
  • Kothari CR. Research Methodology: Methods and Techniques.

Q3 Cardiovascular Adaptation to Exercise and Training

The cardiovascular system supplies oxygen and nutrients to working muscles and removes waste products during activity. Regular endurance training produces long-term changes in the heart, blood vessels and blood. This question asks how the cardiovascular system adapts to repeated exercise (training).

Cardiac adaptations

  • Cardiac hypertrophy — heart wall thickens and the chamber enlarges, mainly the left ventricle.
  • Increased stroke volume (more blood pumped per beat).
  • Lower resting heart rate in trained people.
  • Higher cardiac output during maximal exercise.

Vascular and blood adaptations

  • More capillaries in trained muscles.
  • Better blood flow and oxygen delivery.
  • Increased blood volume and haemoglobin.
  • Lower peripheral resistance.

Rest vs exercise

  • At rest: low heart rate, same cardiac output.
  • In exercise: high stroke volume and cardiac output.
  • Faster return to normal after exercise.

Benefits

  • Higher VO2 max and endurance.
  • Lower blood pressure and heart disease risk.
  • Delayed onset of fatigue.
  • Quicker recovery of heart rate after exercise.

Training -> bigger stroke volume -> higher cardiac output -> better endurance.

Regular exercise makes the heart and circulation more efficient, improving oxygen delivery and endurance.

Recent advances

  • High-intensity interval training (HIIT) improves cardiac function in less time.
  • Cardiac rehabilitation is now routine after a heart attack.

References

  • Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology.
  • ACSM's Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription.
  • McArdle, Katch & Katch, Exercise Physiology.

Q4 Fatigue

Fatigue is a feeling of tiredness and reduced ability to continue work or exercise. It is a normal, protective response of the body to prolonged activity. This question asks about the meaning, types, causes and management of fatigue in exercise.

Definition and types

  • Fatigue — reduced capacity to produce force or keep working.
  • Acute fatigue — occurs during or after one bout, recovers with rest.
  • Chronic fatigue — lasts long, due to overtraining or disease.

Peripheral causes

  • Depletion of ATP and phosphocreatine.
  • Build-up of lactic acid and H+ ions (lower pH).
  • Loss of muscle glycogen.
  • Dehydration and loss of electrolytes.

Central fatigue

  • Starts in the brain and spinal cord.
  • Fewer nerve signals reach the muscles.
  • Affected by motivation, mood and temperature.

Signs

  • Weakness and slower movements.
  • Poor coordination and slow reactions.
  • Reduced performance and early stoppage.
  • Muscle pain and cramps.

Management

  • Adequate rest and sleep.
  • Good diet, hydration and carbohydrates.
  • Graded training and warm-up.
  • Avoid overtraining and take rest days.

Fatigue limits performance but is a normal signal; rest, nutrition and graded training help recovery.

Recent advances

  • Central fatigue is now seen as a major factor in long exercise.
  • Sports drinks and carbohydrate gels are used to delay fatigue.

References

  • Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology.
  • McArdle, Katch & Katch, Exercise Physiology.
  • Powers & Howley, Exercise Physiology.

Q5 Competency-Based Education

Education aims to prepare students for real work and life. Traditionally it focused on finishing the syllabus and passing exams. This question asks about competency-based education (CBE), which focuses on what a student can actually do.

Meaning

  • CBE = teaching and assessing based on competencies.
  • A competency = knowledge + skill + attitude.
  • Focus on the outcome, not the time spent in class.
  • It is also called outcome-based education.

Key features

  • Clear learning outcomes fixed in advance.
  • The student learns at his or her own pace.
  • Assessment is performance-based.
  • Mastery is shown before moving ahead.
  • Standards are clear and measurable.
  • Learning is checked against a fixed standard.

Steps in CBE

  • Define the competencies needed.
  • Design teaching and learning activities.
  • Decide how each competency will be measured.
  • Assess performance in real or simulated tasks.
  • Give feedback and re-assess if needed.

Advantages

  • Produces job-ready and skilled graduates.
  • Makes evaluation fair and clear.
  • Suits clinical subjects like physiotherapy.
  • A limitation is that it is time and resource heavy.

CBE makes education practical and focused on real skills.

Recent advances

  • Use of simulation and virtual patients for practice.
  • Digital portfolios to record and assess competencies.

References

  • Frank JR et al. Competency-based medical education: theory to practice. Medical Teacher.
  • Ten Cate O. Competency-based postgraduate training. Academic Medicine.
  • WHO. Competency-based education in health professions.

Q6 Bloom's Taxonomy

Teachers set learning objectives to decide what students should learn and how to test it. Bloom's taxonomy is a widely used framework that classifies these objectives by level of thinking. This question explains Bloom's taxonomy and its levels.

Meaning

  • Developed by Benjamin Bloom in 1956.
  • Classifies learning objectives from simple to complex.
  • Helps teachers plan lessons and question papers.
  • It moves from lower to higher thinking.
  • A revised version came in 2001.

Three domains

  • Cognitive: thinking and knowledge.
  • Affective: feelings and attitude.
  • Psychomotor: physical skills.
  • The cognitive domain is used most in exams.

Cognitive levels (revised)

  • Remember→Understand→Apply→Analyse→Evaluate→Create
  • Remember: recall facts.
  • Understand: explain ideas.
  • Apply: use knowledge in new situations.
  • Analyse: break the topic into parts.
  • Evaluate: judge and justify.
  • Create: produce something new.
  • Each level builds on the one below.

Importance

  • Guides question paper setting.
  • Covers both simple and higher-order thinking.
  • Helps write clear learning objectives.
  • Useful in teaching physiotherapy skills.

Bloom's taxonomy helps make teaching and assessment balanced and clear.

Recent advances

  • Revised taxonomy keeps "Create" as the highest level.
  • Use of digital tools to design higher-order questions.

References

  • Anderson LW, Krathwohl DR. A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching and Assessing.
  • Bloom BS. Taxonomy of Educational Objectives.
  • Krathwohl DR. A revision of Bloom's taxonomy. Theory into Practice.

Q7 Scales for Gait and Pain Assessment

Assessment is the first step of physiotherapy, and gait and pain are two very common things to measure. Simple, reliable scales help the therapist record findings and track progress. This question asks about scales used to assess gait and pain.

Gait — Basics

  • Gait cycle = one full step pattern of one limb.
  • Stance phase (~60%) — foot on the ground.
  • Swing phase (~40%) — foot off the ground.
  • Gait cycle→Stance→Swing.

Gait Assessment Scales

  • Tinetti Gait and Balance Scale — balance and gait in the elderly.
  • Dynamic Gait Index (DGI) — gait during different tasks.
  • Timed Up and Go (TUG) — time to stand, walk, turn and sit.
  • Functional Gait Assessment — walking with head turns etc.

Pain — Basics

  • Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience.
  • It is measured mainly using self-report scales.

Pain Assessment Scales

  • VAS — Visual Analogue Scale, a 10 cm line.
  • NPRS — Numerical Pain Rating Scale (0-10).
  • VRS — Verbal Rating Scale (mild, moderate, severe).
  • Wong-Baker FACES — faces scale, good for children.
  • McGill Pain Questionnaire — detailed pain quality.

Gait and pain scales are simple, valid tools that help quantify function and pain and guide treatment.

Recent advances

  • Use of wearable sensors and smartphone apps for gait analysis.
  • Digital pain diaries for tracking pain over time.

References

  • Magee DJ. Orthopedic Physical Assessment.
  • Wong DL, Baker CM. Pain in children: comparison of assessment scales. Pediatr Nurs.
  • Tinetti ME. Performance-oriented assessment of mobility problems in elderly patients. J Am Geriatr Soc.

Q8 SPADI (Shoulder Pain and Disability Index)

The shoulder is a very mobile joint, and problems there cause both pain and difficulty in daily tasks. A good outcome measure is needed to record this. This question asks about the SPADI, a tool for assessing shoulder problems.

What is SPADI

  • Full form: Shoulder Pain and Disability Index.
  • A self-administered questionnaire.
  • Developed by Roach et al. (1991).
  • Measures pain and disability of the shoulder.

Structure

  • 13 items in total.
  • 5 items for pain.
  • 8 items for disability.
  • Pain items ask about pain in different situations.
  • Disability items ask about difficulty in daily tasks (e.g. dressing).

Scoring

  • Each item scored on a 0-10 scale.
  • Total score converted to a percentage (0-100).
  • Higher score = more pain and disability.
  • Pain and disability subscores can also be calculated.

Uses and Properties

  • Used in shoulder pain, frozen shoulder, rotator cuff problems.
  • Good reliability and validity.
  • Easy and quick to use.
  • Helps track progress over time.
  • Useful before and after shoulder treatment.

SPADI is a simple, reliable shoulder-specific tool that measures both pain and disability.

Recent advances

  • Translated and validated in many languages.
  • Available as electronic/digital versions.

References

  • Roach KE, et al. Development of a shoulder pain and disability index. Arthritis Care Res. 1991.
  • MacDermid JC, et al. Measuring shoulder pain and disability: the SPADI. J Rheumatol.
  • Magee DJ. Orthopedic Physical Assessment.

Q9 Muscle Plasticity

Skeletal muscle is adaptable and can change its size, strength and fibre type with use or disuse. This ability to change is called muscle plasticity. This question asks about the types and basis of muscle plasticity.

Meaning

  • Muscle plasticity — ability of muscle to change with demand.
  • Changes may be structural, functional or metabolic.
  • Seen with training, disuse and ageing.

Hypertrophy

  • Increase in muscle size due to strength training.
  • More protein and myofibrils in the fibres.
  • Satellite cells help the muscle grow.
  • Muscle protein and strength increase with regular training.

Atrophy

  • Decrease in muscle size from disuse, bed rest or immobilisation.
  • Loss of muscle protein and strength.
  • Common after fractures and long bed rest.
  • Reversed by early mobilisation and exercise.

Fibre type changes

  • Endurance training shifts fibres towards slow (type I).
  • Strength training shifts fibres towards fast (type II).
  • Fibre type is partly genetic.

Other changes

  • More capillaries and mitochondria with endurance training.
  • Better neural control and coordination.
  • Increased muscle strength and endurance.

Muscle plasticity lets muscle adapt to training and disuse, which is the basis of physiotherapy exercise programmes.

Recent advances

  • mTOR signalling studies explain how muscles grow.
  • Satellite cells and stem cells in muscle repair are a major research area.

References

  • McArdle, Katch & Katch, Exercise Physiology.
  • Lieber, Skeletal Muscle Structure, Function and Plasticity.
  • Guyton & Hall, Textbook of Medical Physiology.

Q10 Body Composition

Body composition is the study of how the body is divided into its main parts, such as fat, muscle, bone and water. It tells us the ratio of fat mass to lean (fat-free) mass. This question asks about the meaning of body composition, the common methods used to measure it, and why it matters in physiotherapy.

Definition and Components

  • Body composition = proportion of fat and fat-free (lean) mass in the body.
  • Two main parts: fat mass and fat-free mass (muscle, bone, water, organs).
  • Some body fat is needed for energy and protection, but too much is harmful.

Common Assessment Methods

  • BMI — simple, uses only height and weight.
  • Skinfold thickness — measures fat under the skin with calipers.
  • Bioelectrical impedance (BIA) — uses a small current to estimate fat and water.
  • DEXA and hydrostatic weighing — more accurate but costly.

Clinical Significance in Physiotherapy

  • High fat and low muscle reduce strength and exercise capacity.
  • Helps guide exercise prescription and weight-management advice.
  • Useful to track progress in obesity, diabetes and cardiac rehab.

Factors Affecting Body Composition

  • Age, sex, genetics and diet.
  • Physical activity level and presence of disease.

Measuring body composition helps the physiotherapist plan safe, effective exercise and track the patient's progress.

Recent advances

  • 3D body scanners and smart scales give quick estimates at home.
  • DEXA is now widely used as the gold standard in research.

References

  • Heyward VH, Wagner DR. Applied Body Composition Assessment. Human Kinetics.
  • ACSM's Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription.
  • Gibson AL, et al. Advanced Nutrition and Human Metabolism.

Q11 Influence of Age and Gender in Exercise and Training

The body's response to exercise is influenced by many personal factors, including age and sex. These factors affect strength, endurance and recovery. This question asks how age and gender affect exercise capacity and prescription.

Effects of age

  • Children — lower anaerobic capacity, faster recovery, need play-based exercise.
  • Adults — peak performance usually in the 20s to 30s.
  • Elderly — less muscle mass, strength and VO2 max; slower recovery.

Effects of gender

  • Women generally have less muscle mass and lower strength.
  • Women have a higher body fat percentage.
  • Men have a larger heart and higher haemoglobin.
  • Differences reduce with training.

Physiological reasons

  • Hormones such as testosterone affect muscle growth.
  • Age reduces muscle fibres and tissue elasticity.
  • Heart and lung function fall with age.
  • Women have a lower VO2 max than men.

Exercise prescription

  • Training should be individualised by age and sex.
  • Elderly — low-impact, balance and strength work.
  • Children — avoid heavy resistance training.
  • Women — weight-bearing exercise for bone health.
  • Warm-up and safety are important at all ages.

Age and gender affect exercise response, so programmes must be tailored to each person.

Recent advances

  • Exercise is shown to slow sarcopenia (age-related muscle loss).
  • Research on sex differences in training and injury risk is growing.

References

  • ACSM's Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription.
  • McArdle, Katch & Katch, Exercise Physiology.
  • Powers & Howley, Exercise Physiology.

Q12 Electrical Properties of Muscle and Nerve

Nerve and muscle are called excitable tissues because they can generate and conduct electrical signals. These signals allow nerves to carry messages and muscles to contract. This question asks about the basic electrical properties of nerve and muscle and how they relate to electrotherapy.

Resting Membrane Potential

  • The inside of the cell is negative compared to the outside (about -70 mV).
  • Maintained by the sodium-potassium pump and ion channels.
  • This steady difference is the resting membrane potential.

Action Potential

  • A stimulus opens sodium channels, so sodium enters the cell.
  • Depolarisation makes the inside positive.
  • Then potassium leaves, causing repolarisation back to rest.
  • The signal travels along the nerve or muscle fibre.

Excitability and Threshold

  • Tissue responds only to a stimulus above a certain threshold.
  • The response is all-or-none — either a full impulse or none.
  • A stronger stimulus does not make a bigger impulse, only more frequent ones.

Conductivity and Refractory Period

  • Conductivity = ability to pass the impulse along the fibre.
  • The refractory period is the short time the tissue cannot be excited again.
  • Myelinated nerves conduct faster by saltatory conduction.

These electrical properties explain how nerves control muscles and why electrical stimulation can excite them.

Recent advances

  • Nerve conduction studies are now standard for diagnosing neuropathy.
  • Threshold tracking is used to study nerve excitability in disease.

References

  • Guyton AC, Hall JE. Textbook of Medical Physiology.
  • Cameron MH. Physical Agents in Rehabilitation.
  • Robertson V, et al. Electrotherapy Explained.

Q13 Learning Models for Competency and Instrumentation

In physiotherapy education, students must become competent — able to perform safely and correctly. Learning models help teachers plan this learning, and instrumentation means the tools used to measure it. This question asks about the learning models used for competency and the instruments used to assess it.

Competency and Competency-Based Education

  • Competency = the knowledge, skills and attitude needed to practise safely.
  • Competency-based education focuses on outcomes, not just time spent.
  • Students must show they can actually do the task.

Learning Models

  • Bloom's taxonomy — learning from remembering up to creating.
  • Miller's pyramid — knows, knows how, shows how, does.
  • Dreyfus model — stages from novice to expert.
  • Kolb's cycle — learning by experience and reflection.
  • Gagne's conditions — different outcomes need different teaching.

Instrumentation and Assessment Tools

  • OSCE (Objective Structured Clinical Examination) tests practical skills.
  • Checklists and rubrics give standard scores.
  • Portfolios and logbooks record progress over time.
  • Written exams and viva test knowledge.
  • Feedback and self-assessment help students improve.

Application

  • Models guide teaching, practice and feedback.
  • Instruments give fair, standard scores.

Good learning models and reliable instruments together ensure students become safe, competent physiotherapists.

Recent advances

  • Simulation-based learning is increasingly used for competency.
  • Digital e-portfolios make assessment easier to track.

References

  • Miller GE. The assessment of clinical skills/competence/performance. Acad Med, 1990.
  • Kolb DA. Experiential Learning.
  • Biggs J, Tang C. Teaching for Quality Learning at University.

Q14 International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)

Disability can be understood in different ways, and how we classify it affects treatment. The ICF is a framework developed by the WHO to describe health and disability. This question asks about the ICF model and its components.

What is ICF

  • Full form: International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health.
  • Developed by WHO in 2001.
  • It describes how a person functions in daily life, not just the disease.
  • It is a biopsychosocial model.

Main Components

  • Body Functions and Structures — body systems and parts.
  • Activities — what a person can do (e.g. walking).
  • Participation — involvement in life situations (e.g. work, school).
  • The health condition is the disease or disorder.

Contextual Factors

  • Environmental factors — external, e.g. ramps, family support.
  • Personal factors — internal, e.g. age, coping style.
  • These can act as barriers or facilitators.

Structure and Use

  • Body Functions→Activities→Participation.
  • Used to plan and measure rehabilitation.
  • Helps set goals and choose outcome measures.
  • Used in clinical, research and policy settings.

The ICF gives a complete, holistic picture of a patient's health and disability beyond just the diagnosis.

Recent advances

  • ICF-based core sets developed for specific conditions.
  • Increasing use of ICF in electronic health records.

References

  • World Health Organization. International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF). Geneva: WHO; 2001.
  • Ustun TB, et al. Measuring Health and Disability: Manual for WHO Disability Assessment Schedule.
  • Dutton M. Dutton's Orthopaedic Examination, Evaluation and Intervention.

Q15 Neuromuscular Electrical Stimulation (NMES) and Its Effects on Body Systems

Electrotherapy uses electrical currents for treatment, and NMES (neuromuscular electrical stimulation) is one common method. NMES applies current through electrodes to make muscles contract. This question asks about the main effects of NMES on different body systems.

Effects on the Muscular System

  • Produces muscle contraction without voluntary effort.
  • Helps prevent muscle atrophy and improve strength.
  • Improves muscle endurance and can reduce muscle spasm.
  • Helps maintain muscle bulk when a limb is immobilised.

Effects on Circulation and Vascular System

  • Muscle pumping improves blood and lymph flow.
  • Helps reduce swelling (oedema).
  • May improve venous return to the heart.

Effects on the Nervous System

  • Stimulates motor nerves to contract muscle.
  • Can stimulate sensory nerves to reduce pain (gate theory).
  • May help retrain movement after stroke.

Effects on Skin and Other Systems

  • Mild skin redness under the electrodes is common.
  • Can improve joint range and reduce stiffness in some cases.

Precautions

  • Avoid use over the heart, neck and pregnant abdomen.
  • Not used over cancer tissue or thrombosed veins.

NMES mainly acts on muscle and circulation, so it is useful for weakness, swelling and pain.

Recent advances

  • NMES is used for early rehabilitation after knee surgery.
  • Portable home NMES devices are now widely available.

References

  • Cameron MH. Physical Agents in Rehabilitation.
  • Robertson V, et al. Electrotherapy Explained.
  • Prentice WE. Therapeutic Modalities in Rehabilitation.

Q16 Methods of Data Collection

Data collection is the process of gathering information for a study or an assessment. It is an important step, because the quality of the data decides the quality of the results. This question asks about the different methods used to collect data.

Meaning

  • Data = facts, figures or responses that are collected.
  • Data collection = the systematic gathering of data.
  • Two sources: primary (first-hand) and secondary (already published).

Main methods

  • Interview: face to face or telephonic.
  • Questionnaire: a written set of questions.
  • Observation: watching and recording behaviour.
  • Examination/tests: measuring physical functions.
  • Focus group discussion.
  • Records: hospital files, registers and reports.

Primary vs secondary

  • Primary: collected by the researcher directly (surveys, interviews).
  • Secondary: taken from existing sources (books, journals, records).
  • Primary is fresh but costly; secondary is quick but may be old.

Steps in data collection

  • Plan what data is needed.
  • Prepare and test the tool.
  • Collect the data from subjects.
  • Record and check for errors.

Qualities of a good method

  • Reliable and valid.
  • Simple and easy to use.
  • Gives complete and accurate data.

The right method is chosen based on the study aim, time and cost.

Recent advances

  • Use of online forms and mobile apps for surveys.
  • Wearable sensors and digital records for real-time data.

References

  • Kothari CR. Research Methodology: Methods and Techniques.
  • Bowling A. Research Methods in Health.
  • Ahuja R. Research Methods.

Q17 Tyler's Principles of Curriculum Development

Curriculum planning decides what students should learn and how it is taught and tested. Ralph Tyler gave a simple model for this in 1949. This question asks about Tyler's principles of curriculum development and their use in physiotherapy education.

Background

  • Given by Ralph Tyler in his book *Basic Principles of Curriculum and Instruction* (1949).
  • Aims to plan and evaluate a curriculum in a logical way.
  • One of the classic models of curriculum design.

The Four Fundamental Questions

  • What educational purposes (objectives) should the school seek?
  • What learning experiences can help reach them?
  • How can these experiences be organised effectively?
  • How can we evaluate whether the aims are met?

Objectives and Learning Experiences

  • Objectives come from learners, society and the subject.
  • Learning experiences must match the objectives.
  • The learner must practise the desired behaviour.

Organisation and Evaluation

  • Experiences are organised with continuity, sequence and integration.
  • Evaluation checks whether the objectives are achieved.
  • Results are used to improve the curriculum.
  • Objectives, experiences and evaluation must all match each other.

Tyler's model gives a clear step-by-step way to design and improve a physiotherapy curriculum.

Recent advances

  • Tyler's model is combined with outcome-based education today.
  • Competency frameworks apply Tyler's steps to clinical skills.

References

  • Tyler RW. Basic Principles of Curriculum and Instruction. University of Chicago Press, 1949.
  • Ornstein AC, Hunkins FP. Curriculum: Foundations, Principles and Issues.
