Q1 Professional Values of Physiotherapy

Physiotherapy is a health profession that restores and improves movement and function. It deals with people who are in pain, weak or disabled. So a physiotherapist needs more than technical skill. They also need a clear set of moral values. This question asks about the core professional values of physiotherapy and the ethics behind them.

What is a Profession?

  • A profession is more than a job.
  • It has a special body of knowledge.
  • It has formal education and a licence.
  • It puts the public interest first, not its own.
  • This deal between the profession and society is the social contract.

Defining Professional Values

  • Professional values are beliefs that guide how a therapist behaves.
  • They answer "what kind of therapist should I be?"
  • They are shown in daily actions.
  • World Physiotherapy (WCPT) and APTA list these values.

Core Values of Physiotherapy

  • Accountability – taking responsibility for one's actions and results.
  • Altruism – putting the patient's needs before one's own.
  • Compassion and caring – understanding the patient and acting kindly.
  • Excellence – using current knowledge and knowing one's limits.
  • Integrity – being truthful and fair.
  • Professional duty and social responsibility – serving the patient and society.
  • Newer values: collaboration and inclusion.

Ethical Principles

  • Autonomy – respect the patient's own choice (informed consent).
  • Beneficence – do good, choose the best treatment.
  • Non-maleficence – do no harm.
  • Justice – be fair and treat everyone equally.
  • Fidelity and veracity – keep promises and tell the truth.
  • These principles can clash; the therapist must reason.

Codes and Standards

  • WCPT gives international ethical principles and practice standards.
  • IAP gives India's code of ethics and practice guidelines.
  • APTA gives a detailed code of ethics and core values.

Ethical Decision-Making

  • Ethics is a step-by-step process, not just rules.
  • Steps: recognise the issue -> gather facts -> identify values -> weigh options -> act and document -> review.
  • Codes do not cover every case; seek guidance when unsure.
  • Example: deciding whether to continue a painful exercise or to obtain consent before manual therapy.

Patient-Centred Care

  • Treat the patient as a person, not a diagnosis.
  • Ask about their goals and values.
  • Respect culture, language and religion.
  • These goals become the basis of the physiotherapy plan.

Scope of Practice and Lifelong Learning

  • Work only within one's training and competence.
  • Refer or consult when the problem is outside it.
  • Keep learning through CPD.
  • Staying current is an ethical duty, not a choice.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Assessment – values shape informed consent and respectful handling during examination and outcome testing.
  • Treatment planning – beneficence and non-maleficence guide the choice and progression of interventions such as exercise or manual therapy.
  • Patient management – accountability and integrity govern documentation, goal-setting and discharge decisions.

The core values of physiotherapy guide the therapist in every action. They are shown through ethics, patient-centred care and good conduct. They are the real reason the public trusts the profession.

Recent advances

  • Inclusion and equity are now listed as core values.
  • Collaboration with the team and the patient is given more weight.
  • New standards cover digital ethics and social media use.
  • Growing focus on therapist wellbeing and moral distress.

References

1. American Physical Therapy Association. Core Values for the Physical Therapist and Physical Therapist Assistant. APTA, 2021.

2. World Physiotherapy. Policy statement: Ethical principles and responsibilities. London, 2023.

3. Indian Association of Physiotherapists. Physiotherapy Practice Guidelines. IAP, 2020.

4. Beauchamp TL, Childress JF. Principles of Biomedical Ethics. 8th ed. Oxford, 2019.

Q2 Designing a Research Study

Research is a systematic process of asking questions and finding reliable answers. In physiotherapy, research is the base of evidence-based practice. It shows which treatments work, for whom, and at what cost. This answer explains how a physiotherapy research study is designed, step by step.

Research Question

  • A study starts with a clear research question.
  • It should not be too broad or too narrow.
  • Good questions follow the PICO format.
  • PICO = Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome.
  • PICOT adds Time (the follow-up period).

Literature Review

  • Review what is already known on the topic.
  • Use databases like PubMed, CINAHL, PEDro and Cochrane.
  • Find the knowledge gap the new study will fill.
  • Avoid repeating work already done by others.
  • A good review sharpens the question and design.

Hypothesis

  • A hypothesis is a testable prediction.
  • It links the independent and dependent variables.
  • The null hypothesis says there is no difference.
  • It must be set before data collection starts.
  • It may be directional or non-directional.

Study Design

  • Decide if the study is observational or experimental.
  • RCT is best for testing treatment effects.
  • Cohort, case-control and cross-sectional are observational types.
  • Qualitative designs explore patients' experiences of therapy.
  • The design must match the research question.

Sampling and Sample Size

  • Sampling selects a subset of the population.
  • Probability sampling allows generalisation.
  • Non-probability (convenience) sampling is common in physiotherapy clinics.
  • Sample size is fixed by power analysis (power = 0.80).
  • Small samples risk a Type II error.

Variables and Outcome Measures

  • Independent variable = the treatment or exposure.
  • Dependent variable = the outcome measured.
  • A confounder distorts the true relationship.
  • Outcomes need reliability and validity.
  • Use physiotherapy scales (e.g. NPRS, ODI) with a known MCID.

Randomisation and Blinding

  • Randomisation allocates participants by chance.
  • It balances known and unknown factors.
  • Blinding hides group allocation from people.
  • Blinded outcome assessment is vital in physiotherapy.

Bias and Confounding

  • Bias is systematic error that misleads results.
  • Types: selection, performance, detection and attrition bias.
  • Confounders are handled by design or by analysis.

Ethics and Data Analysis

  • Ethics committee approval is compulsory.
  • Follow ICMR 2017 guidelines in India.
  • Informed consent must be free and voluntary.
  • Fix the analysis plan before data collection.
  • Intention-to-treat analysis is preferred.
  • Report using CONSORT or STROBE.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Builds the research evidence base for physiotherapy interventions such as exercise, manual therapy and electrotherapy.
  • Trains clinicians to appraise RCTs and apply findings to individual patient care.
  • Guides physiotherapy students in designing audits, case studies and clinical trials.

A good study moves from a clear question to a suitable design, controlled bias and honest reporting. Every step must be planned carefully in advance.

Recent advances

  • SMART and adaptive trial designs are growing.
  • Registry-based RCTs make trials cheaper.
  • The Declaration of Helsinki was revised in 2024.

References

1. Portney LG, Watkins MP. Foundations of Clinical Research. 4th ed. F.A. Davis; 2020.

2. Hulley SB, et al. Designing Clinical Research. 4th ed. Lippincott; 2013.

3. ICMR. National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research. 2017.

4. Schulz KF, et al. CONSORT 2010 Statement. BMJ. 2010;340:c332.

Q3 Cardiovascular Adaptation to Exercise and Training

Exercise is a strong stimulus for the cardiovascular system, which must deliver more oxygen to working muscles. Its responses are divided into acute responses during a single bout and chronic adaptations after weeks to months of training. These changes affect how exercise is prescribed and monitored. This answer describes the acute responses and the long-term central and peripheral adaptations to endurance training.

Acute responses to a single bout

  • Heart rate rises before exercise (anticipatory rise) due to vagal withdrawal.
  • Heart rate then increases linearly with workload up to maximal.
  • Maximal heart rate is estimated as 220 minus age.
  • Stroke volume rises via the Frank–Starling mechanism and greater contractility.

Cardiac output and blood pressure

  • Cardiac output (Q = HR × SV) rises from about 5 to 20–25 L/min.
  • Systolic blood pressure rises with intensity; diastolic stays the same or falls.
  • The rate–pressure product (HR × systolic BP) shows myocardial oxygen demand.
  • Muscle blood flow rises from about 20% of cardiac output to 80% at maximal exercise.
  • Monitoring HR and BP during exercise is standard practice in cardiac rehabilitation.

The Fick equation and oxygen uptake

  • V̇O₂ = cardiac output × arteriovenous oxygen difference (a-vO₂ diff).
  • V̇O₂max = (HRmax × SVmax) × (CaO₂ − CvO₂).
  • Maximal heart rate is not increased by training; it is age-determined.
  • Stroke volume is the most plastic determinant of aerobic capacity.

Central adaptations to training

  • Eccentric cardiac hypertrophy (chamber enlargement) raises stroke volume.
  • Resting and submaximal bradycardia develops (40–60 beats/min in athletes).
  • Blood volume expands by about 10–20%.
  • Red cell mass and haemoglobin rise modestly.

Peripheral adaptations to training

  • Capillary density and angiogenesis increase, shortening diffusion distance.
  • Mitochondrial content and oxidative enzymes such as citrate synthase increase.
  • There is a fibre-type shift toward a more oxidative phenotype (IIx→IIa).
  • Improved oxygen extraction widens the a-vO₂ difference.

The athlete's heart

  • Morganroth hypothesis: endurance causes eccentric and strength causes concentric hypertrophy.
  • Physiological hypertrophy is reversible and function is normal.
  • Pathological hypertrophy shows fibrosis and dysfunction.
  • Wall thickness of 13–15 mm is a diagnostic grey zone.

Detraining and exercise prescription

  • Detraining: V̇O₂max falls about 7% in 3 weeks and about 16% by 8 weeks.
  • The early loss is from reduced stroke volume and blood volume.
  • Adaptations are best preserved by keeping training intensity.
  • Use the FITT-VP framework; ACSM advises 150 min/week of moderate aerobic exercise.
  • Adjust heart-rate targets in patients taking beta-blockers.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Acute HR and BP responses are used to set and monitor safe aerobic training zones in cardiac and pulmonary rehabilitation.
  • Understanding central and peripheral adaptations guides the progression of endurance exercise in patients with cardiovascular or respiratory disease.
  • Detraining principles support early mobilisation and long-term maintenance programmes in chronic disease.

Exercise causes fast acute cardiovascular adjustments and lasting central and peripheral adaptations that together raise V̇O₂max. Understanding these changes guides safe and effective exercise prescription.

Recent advances

  • Blood-volume expansion alone may not explain training gains; cardiac and mitochondrial changes matter more.
  • Heart-rate-variability-guided training is used to individualise endurance programmes.
  • Cardiac MRI has sharpened the difference between physiological and pathological hypertrophy.

References

1. McArdle WD, Katch FI, Katch VL. Exercise Physiology. 8th ed. Wolters Kluwer, 2015.

2. Kenney WL, Wilmore JH, Costill DL. Physiology of Sport and Exercise. 7th ed. Human Kinetics, 2020.

3. ACSM's Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription. 11th ed. Wolters Kluwer, 2021.

4. Fagard R. Athlete's heart. Heart, 2003.

Q4 Fatigue

Fatigue is a common but often misunderstood concept in exercise physiology. It means the exercise-induced decline in the ability of a muscle to produce force or power. Fatigue is now seen as multifactorial, arising at many sites along the pathway from brain to muscle. This answer defines fatigue and explains its central and peripheral mechanisms, task dependency and measurement.

Defining fatigue

  • Fatigue is any exercise-induced reduction in force or power.
  • It is a graded process that starts early, not only at task failure.
  • Fatigue is different from weakness, which is present at rest.
  • Fatigue is measured as a fall from the person's own baseline.

Central fatigue

  • Central fatigue is a fall in voluntary activation.
  • It involves reduced drive from the motor cortex.
  • Spinal changes include altered motor-neuron excitability and presynaptic inhibition.
  • Feedback from group III/IV muscle afferents inhibits motor output.
  • Sleep loss, heat and psychological stress increase central fatigue.

Peripheral fatigue

  • Peripheral fatigue occurs at or distal to the neuromuscular junction.
  • Neuromuscular transmission and sarcolemmal conduction are impaired.
  • Calcium release and reuptake from the sarcoplasmic reticulum are disturbed.
  • H⁺, inorganic phosphate, lactate and reactive oxygen species accumulate.
  • Phosphocreatine and glycogen are depleted.

Sites along the motor pathway

  • CNS: reduced motor cortical output.
  • Motor neuron: lower firing rates and increased presynaptic inhibition.
  • Neuromuscular junction: impaired transmission.
  • Sarcolemma and T-tubules: conduction failure from potassium build-up.
  • Cross-bridges: Pi and H⁺ inhibit the actin–myosin interaction.

Task dependency

  • There is no single cause of fatigue.
  • The dominant mechanism depends on the task.
  • Short maximal contractions of small muscles cause mainly peripheral fatigue.
  • Prolonged whole-body exercise in heat causes more central fatigue.
  • Findings from one model cannot be generalised to another.

Fatigue in disease

  • Multiple sclerosis: demyelination, impaired central conduction and deconditioning.
  • ME/CFS: disabling fatigue, post-exertional malaise and orthostatic intolerance.
  • Long COVID: overlaps with ME/CFS and shows impaired oxygen extraction.
  • Graded exercise must be cautious to avoid post-exertional malaise.
  • Disease fatigue mechanisms differ from healthy exercise-induced fatigue.

Measurement and recovery

  • Maximal voluntary contraction shows the fall in peak force.
  • Surface EMG shows changes in amplitude and median frequency.
  • Interpolated twitch and transcranial magnetic stimulation reveal the activation deficit.
  • Phosphocreatine recovers in minutes; glycogen takes hours to days.
  • Central fatigue recovers with normal temperature and neurotransmitter balance.
  • Fatigability testing guides safe progression of resistance and endurance training in rehabilitation.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Fatigue assessment (MVC, EMG, twitch) informs safe dosing and progression of exercise in neurological and post-illness rehabilitation.
  • Distinguishing central from peripheral fatigue guides treatment: activation/motor training versus graded peripheral loading.
  • In MS, ME/CFS and long COVID, pacing and energy-conservation strategies are core physiotherapy management.

Fatigue is a task-dependent, multifactorial process with central and peripheral components. Recognising the dominant mechanism helps guide training and rehabilitation.

Recent advances

  • Fatigue is now viewed as an integrative, homeostatic "governor" rather than a rigid dichotomy.
  • Invasive studies argue against simple deconditioning in ME/CFS and long COVID.
  • Two-day exercise testing can demonstrate post-exertional malaise.

References

1. Gandevia SC. Spinal and supraspinal factors in human muscle fatigue. Physiological Reviews, 2001.

2. Taylor JL, et al. Neural contributions to muscle fatigue. Med Sci Sports Exerc, 2016.

3. McArdle WD, Katch FI, Katch VL. Exercise Physiology. 8th ed. Wolters Kluwer, 2015.

4. Joseph P, et al. Exercise pathophysiology in ME/CFS and PASC. Chest, 2023.

Q5 Competency-Based Education

Competency-based education (CBE) is a modern approach to training health professionals. Here, learning is fixed and time is flexible. A learner moves ahead only after showing competence. This answer explains CBE and how it applies to physiotherapy education.

Meaning of Competence

  • Competency = an observable ability of a professional.
  • It combines knowledge, skills and attitudes.
  • It grows along a path from novice to expert.
  • A milestone marks a stage on this path.
  • An EPA is a task trusted to a learner.

Core Principles

  • Outcomes define the curriculum.
  • Progression depends on shown competence, not time spent.
  • Assessment is continuous and workplace-based.
  • The goal is safe, unsupervised physiotherapy practice.
  • Learning is individualised.

Comparison with Traditional Education

  • The traditional model uses fixed time and exams.
  • It assumes exposure plus time gives competence.
  • CBE starts with the end in mind.
  • CBE is more demanding but more equitable.
  • Every graduate meets the same standard.

Competency Frameworks

  • CanMEDS 2015 has 7 roles, with Medical Expert central.
  • WHO Rehabilitation Competency Framework (2020) has 5 domains.
  • World Physiotherapy Framework (2021) lists 8 domains.
  • Frameworks organise many abilities into teachable domains.

Why CBE Matters

  • Reduces variation between graduates.
  • Puts patient safety first.
  • Makes learning goals transparent.
  • Supports lifelong learning and growth.

Outcomes versus Process

  • CBE is a form of outcome-based education.
  • "Product defines process" (Harden).
  • Learning outcomes replace learning objectives.
  • Outcomes state what a graduate can actually do.

Assessment in CBE

  • EPAs are tasks trusted to a learner.
  • Entrustment levels run from observation to independent practice.
  • Workplace-based assessment on clinical placements gives regular feedback.
  • Programmatic assessment combines many low-stakes tests.
  • Portfolios and committees guide big decisions.

Entrustment Levels

  • Level 1: allowed to observe only.
  • Level 2: allowed under direct supervision.
  • Level 3: allowed under indirect supervision.
  • Level 4: allowed to practise independently.
  • Level 5: allowed to supervise others.

Physiotherapy Standards

  • World Physiotherapy sets an entry-to-practice threshold.
  • Canada's Competency Profile (2017) has 7 domains.
  • The US uses the CPI tool.
  • India is aligning curricula with graduate competencies.
  • EPAs link classrooms to real practice.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Sets entry-to-practice standards so graduates can assess and treat patients safely and independently.
  • Uses EPAs and portfolios to sign off core skills like gait training, manual therapy and exercise prescription.
  • Supports lifelong learning and re-certification throughout a physiotherapist's career.

CBE focuses on what a graduate can do, not how long they studied. It aims to assure safe practice for all patients.

Recent advances

  • The CBEPT framework now covers the whole learner continuum.
  • Hybrid competency-plus-EPA models are emerging.
  • Simulation is used to check threshold competence.

References

1. Frank JR, et al. Competency-based medical education: theory to practice. Medical Teacher. 2010.

2. ten Cate O. Entrustability of professional activities. Medical Teacher. 2005.

3. World Physiotherapy. Physiotherapist Education Framework. 2021.

4. Timmerberg JF, et al. CBE and Practice in Physical Therapy. Physical Therapy. 2022.

Q6 Bloom's Taxonomy

Bloom's Taxonomy classifies educational objectives by difficulty. It was published in 1956 by Benjamin Bloom. It gives teachers a common language for learning goals. This answer explains the taxonomy and its use in physiotherapy education.

Original Taxonomy (1956)

  • Six levels, from simple to complex.
  • Knowledge — recalling facts.
  • Comprehension — understanding meaning.
  • Application — using knowledge in new situations.
  • Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation — higher-order skills.

Revised Taxonomy (2001)

  • Revised by Anderson and Krathwohl.
  • Categories changed from nouns to verbs.
  • Order: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create.
  • Added a knowledge dimension (factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive).
  • It is now a two-dimensional framework.

Levels in Detail

  • Remember — recall facts and terms.
  • Understand — explain ideas in your own words.
  • Apply — use information in a new situation.
  • Analyze — draw connections between ideas.
  • Evaluate — justify a stand or decision.
  • Create — produce new or original work.

Three Domains of Learning

  • Cognitive — thinking and knowledge.
  • Affective — attitudes, values and feelings.
  • Psychomotor — physical skills and movement.
  • Each domain has its own levels.

Affective and Psychomotor Levels

  • Affective: Receiving, Responding, Valuing, Organizing, Characterizing.
  • Psychomotor (Simpson): Perception, Set, Guided response, Mechanism, Complex overt response, Adaptation, Origination.
  • Affective is key for professionalism and empathy.
  • Psychomotor covers manual therapy and exercise skills.

Action Verbs

  • Remember: define, list, name.
  • Understand: explain, describe, summarise.
  • Apply: demonstrate, use, implement.
  • Analyze: compare, differentiate.
  • Evaluate: justify, critique. Create: design, plan.

Lower- and Higher-Order Thinking

  • Lower-order: remember, understand, apply.
  • Higher-order: analyze, evaluate, create.
  • Clinical reasoning in physiotherapy needs higher-order thinking.
  • Exams should test both levels.

Writing Learning Outcomes

  • Name an observable behaviour.
  • State the conditions of performance.
  • State the criterion for success.
  • Match the verb to the right level.

Use in Physiotherapy

  • Writes measurable learning outcomes.
  • Sequences content from simple to complex.
  • Supports constructive alignment of teaching and testing.
  • Audits exams for higher-order questions.
  • Guides feedback and progression.
  • Helps teachers set the right difficulty.

Remember -> Understand -> Apply -> Analyze -> Evaluate -> Create

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Writes measurable learning outcomes for practical physiotherapy skills and clinical reasoning.
  • Guides exam and viva questions toward clinical decision-making rather than rote recall.
  • Helps educators sequence psychomotor skills from simple to complex during clinical training.

Bloom's Taxonomy helps educators plan teaching and assessment at the right level. In physiotherapy it pushes students beyond rote recall toward clinical reasoning.

Recent advances

  • Miller's Pyramid and SOLO are used alongside Bloom's.
  • AI tools now classify and generate exam questions by level.
  • Studies show exam papers still favour recall items.

References

1. Bloom BS. Taxonomy of Educational Objectives, Handbook I. David McKay; 1956.

2. Anderson LW, Krathwohl DR. A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing. Longman; 2001.

3. Krathwohl DR. A revision of Bloom's taxonomy: an overview. Theory into Practice. 2002.

4. Simpson EJ. The Classification of Educational Objectives in the Psychomotor Domain. 1972.

Q7 Scales for Gait and Pain Assessment

Gait and pain are two of the most basic things a physiotherapist assesses. Gait is how a person walks, and pain is the most common reason patients come for treatment. Both must be measured to set goals and track progress. This question asks about the scales used to assess gait and pain.

Gait: Basic Terms

  • Gait is the manner of walking.
  • The gait cycle is from one heel strike to the next of the same foot.
  • Stance phase is about 60% of the cycle.
  • Swing phase is about 40%.
  • Normal walking speed is about 1.1–1.4 m/s.

Gait Parameters

  • Temporal – cadence, stride time, step time, stance and swing time.
  • Spatial – step length, stride length, step width, foot angle.
  • Derived – walking speed and symmetry.
  • Speed below 0.8 m/s means higher fall risk.

Gait and Balance Scales

  • Dynamic Gait Index (DGI) – 8 items, maximum 24.
  • Functional Gait Assessment (FGA) – 10 items, maximum 30; adds narrow base, eyes closed and backward walking.
  • Tinetti POMA – balance (16) plus gait (12), total 28.
  • Berg Balance Scale (BBS) – 14 items, maximum 56; standard for balance.
  • Timed Up and Go (TUG) – time to stand, walk 3 m, turn and sit; ≥13.5 s means fall risk.
  • 10-metre walk test (10MWT) and 6-minute walk test (6MWT) – speed and endurance.

Pain: Definition and Types

  • Pain is "an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience".
  • It is subjective; the patient's report is the standard.
  • By duration: acute and chronic (more than 3 months).
  • By mechanism: nociceptive, neuropathic and nociplastic.

Unidimensional Pain Tools

  • Measure intensity only; quick and easy.
  • Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) – 10 cm line.
  • Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) – 0 to 10, most widely used.
  • Verbal Rating Scale (VRS) – none, mild, moderate, severe.
  • Wong-Baker FACES – for children and those who cannot use numbers.

Multidimensional Pain Tools

  • Measure the full experience of pain.
  • McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ) – word descriptors for pain quality.
  • Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) – severity plus interference in daily life.
  • Others: painDETECT, Pain Catastrophizing Scale, Tampa Scale.

Psychometric Properties

  • Validity – does it measure the right thing?
  • Reliability – is it consistent (ICC above 0.75 is good)?
  • MDC – smallest real change beyond measurement error.
  • MCID – smallest change the patient finds important.
  • Watch for floor and ceiling effects.
  • In practice, compare the patient's change against MDC and MCID to decide if treatment is working.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Assessment – DGI, BBS and TUG screen fall risk and mobility, while NRS or VAS tracks pain each session.
  • Treatment planning – scores set measurable gait and pain goals and guide progression of exercise and gait re-education.
  • Outcome measurement – interpret change against MDC and MCID to judge whether the physiotherapy programme is effective.

Gait and pain are measured with valid, reliable scales. Gait uses timed and observational tests; pain uses intensity and multidimensional tools. Correct choice and interpretation against MDC and MCID make assessment evidence-based.

Recent advances

  • Wearables and apps allow real-time (ecological) pain and gait measurement.
  • PROMIS uses item-response theory and computerised adaptive testing.
  • Low-cost inertial sensors and video gait analysis are now common.
  • Nociplastic pain is now seen as a separate mechanism.

References

1. Magee DJ. Orthopedic Physical Assessment. 7th ed. Elsevier, 2021.

2. Wrisley DM, et al. Functional gait assessment. Phys Ther. 2004;84(10):906–918.

3. Tinetti ME. Performance-oriented assessment of mobility. J Am Geriatr Soc. 1986;34(2):119–126.

4. Melzack R. The McGill Pain Questionnaire. Pain. 1975;1(3):277–299.

Q8 SPADI (Shoulder Pain and Disability Index)

Shoulder problems are very common in physiotherapy. They cause pain, stiffness and loss of function. Doctors need the patient's own view of the shoulder problem. The SPADI was made for this purpose. This question asks about the SPADI — its purpose, structure, scoring and use.

Purpose

  • Measures pain and disability of the shoulder.
  • It is self-reported (no clinician rating).
  • It is region-specific for the shoulder.
  • Used in rotator cuff problems, frozen shoulder and shoulder arthritis.
  • Complements, but does not replace, physical examination.

Development

  • Developed by Roach and colleagues in 1991.
  • First tested on 37 male patients with shoulder pain.
  • Originally used a VAS; later changed to a 0–10 numeric scale.
  • Reported good internal consistency (alpha 0.86–0.95).

Structure

  • 13 items in two parts.
  • Pain subscale – 5 items (pain at worst, lying on side, reaching high, touching neck, pushing).
  • Disability subscale – 8 items (washing hair, washing back, dressing, carrying, reaching).
  • Each item scored 0–10.
  • Takes only 2–3 minutes to complete.

Scoring

  • Score is a percentage (0–100); higher means worse.
  • Method 1: sum 13 items ÷ 130 × 100.
  • Method 2: pain ÷ 50 × 100 and disability ÷ 80 × 100, then average.
  • Method 2 gives equal weight to pain and disability (preferred).
  • A fall in score means improvement.

Administration

  • Self-administered (paper, digital, interview or phone).
  • No equipment or clinician time needed.
  • Good for busy clinics, repeated use and telehealth.

Psychometric Properties

  • Internal consistency high (alpha 0.86–0.96).
  • Test–retest reliability good (ICC 0.84–0.95).
  • Construct validity – two-factor structure; correlates with ASES.
  • Responsiveness moderate to good.
  • MDC about 18 points and MCID about 13 points.
  • Modern Rasch analysis says treat the two subscales separately.

Comparison with Other Scales

  • DASH – whole upper limb, 30 items; broader but longer.
  • QuickDASH – 11 items, shorter form.
  • Constant score – clinician-rated, includes strength and range of motion.
  • SPADI is shorter and more shoulder-specific than DASH.

Clinical Use

  • Used at baseline, for goals, progress and outcome.
  • Easy to explain: "your score improved from 60% to 40%".
  • Hindi and other Indian language versions exist.
  • In physiotherapy, it supports discharge planning once the score improves by more than the MCID.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Assessment – quickly captures the patient's pain and disability in rotator cuff, frozen shoulder and arthritis.
  • Treatment planning – the baseline score sets realistic goals and guides progression of manual therapy and exercise.
  • Outcome measurement – compare the change against MDC (~18) and MCID (~13) to decide if the shoulder programme is helping.

The SPADI is a short, reliable and widely used shoulder questionnaire. It measures pain and disability from the patient's view. It is best scored as two subscales and interpreted with MDC and MCID.

Recent advances

  • Rasch analysis supports scoring pain and disability separately.
  • Growing digital administration through apps and registries.
  • Core outcome sets for shoulder disorders include pain and function.
  • Translated into many languages, including Indian ones.

References

1. Roach KE, et al. Development of a shoulder pain and disability index. Arthritis Care Res. 1991;4(4):143–149.

2. Williams JW Jr, et al. Measuring shoulder function with the SPADI. J Rheumatol. 1995;22(4):727–732.

3. MacDermid JC, et al. The SPADI demonstrates factor, construct and longitudinal validity. BMC Musculoskelet Disord. 2006;7:12.

Q9 Muscle Plasticity

Skeletal muscle is highly adaptable and changes with the demands placed on it. It can alter its size, fibre type, organelles and proteins — a property called muscle plasticity. This allows strength gains with resistance training and fatigue resistance with endurance training. This answer defines plasticity and describes fibre types, hypertrophy, atrophy and its clinical uses.

Defining muscle plasticity

  • Plasticity is the ability of muscle to change its phenotype.
  • It includes changes in fibre cross-sectional area.
  • It includes changes in fibre-type composition (myosin heavy chain).
  • It includes changes in organelles and protein content.
  • Adaptation is specific to the stimulus.

Fibre types and their transitions

  • Type I fibres are slow, oxidative and fatigue-resistant.
  • Type IIa fibres are fast, moderately oxidative and fatigue-resistant.
  • Type IIx fibres are fast, glycolytic and easily fatigued.
  • Endurance training shifts IIx→IIa and increases oxidative fibres.
  • Disuse shifts fibres toward faster types.

Neural versus morphological adaptation

  • Early strength gains are mainly from neural adaptation.
  • This includes better motor-unit recruitment and firing.
  • Reduced antagonist co-contraction also helps.
  • Hypertrophy becomes dominant after several weeks.
  • Both processes matter clinically: neural change gives early gains and hypertrophy gives long-term gains.
  • In rehabilitation, early neural gains justify starting strengthening before hypertrophy is measurable.

Hypertrophy

  • Hypertrophy is an increase in fibre cross-sectional area.
  • It is driven by mechanical tension, metabolic stress and muscle damage.
  • mTORC1 is the central signalling pathway.
  • The IGF-1–PI3K–Akt–mTOR axis promotes protein synthesis.
  • Myostatin is a negative regulator of muscle mass.

Satellite cells

  • Satellite cells are muscle stem cells under the basal lamina.
  • They activate, divide and fuse with existing fibres.
  • Fusion adds new myonuclei (myonuclear accretion).
  • Satellite cells are needed for full long-term adaptation.
  • Hormones such as testosterone and growth hormone promote satellite-cell activity, which is why hypertrophy is slow and needs repeated loading.

Atrophy

  • Atrophy is loss of muscle mass from reduced loading or disease.
  • Protein degradation exceeds protein synthesis.
  • The ubiquitin–proteasome system becomes more active.
  • Immobilisation causes rapid atrophy, especially of type I fibres.
  • Atrophy is reversible with remobilisation and loading.

Clinical implications

  • Progressive resistance training builds muscle (loads about 60–85% of 1RM).
  • Early mobilisation prevents disuse atrophy after injury.
  • Blood-flow-restriction training gives gains with low loads.
  • Progressive resistance training is the best treatment for sarcopenia.
  • After neurological injury, target neural adaptation first.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Muscle plasticity is the scientific basis of therapeutic exercise, from progressive resistance training to endurance conditioning.
  • It guides early mobilisation to limit disuse atrophy and blood-flow-restriction training when high loads are unsafe.
  • After neurological injury, target neural adaptation first, then build mass and strength with progressive loading.

Muscle plasticity lets skeletal muscle remodel its size, fibre type and metabolism in response to demand. This is the scientific basis of therapeutic exercise.

Recent advances

  • Satellite-cell secretory signalling is needed for full adaptation.
  • The myostatin–follistatin–BMP axis is a key regulator of muscle mass.
  • Single-fibre proteomics has revealed fibre-specific training responses.

References

1. Schiaffino S, Reggiani C, et al. Molecular mechanisms of skeletal muscle hypertrophy. J Neuromuscul Dis, 2021.

2. Plotkin DL, et al. Muscle fiber type transitions with exercise training. Sports, 2021.

3. Hughes DC, Ellefsen S, Baar K. Adaptations to endurance and strength training. Cold Spring Harb Perspect Med, 2018.

4. McArdle WD, Katch FI, Katch VL. Exercise Physiology. 8th ed. Wolters Kluwer, 2015.

Q10 Body Composition

Body composition means the different parts that together make up total body weight. It mainly divides the body into fat mass and fat-free mass. It is more useful than weight or BMI because it shows the *quality* of the body, not just its size. This answer describes the compartment models, fat distribution, the main assessment methods and why body composition matters in physiotherapy.

Definition and Levels of Organisation

  • Body composition is the proportion of fat and lean tissue in the body.
  • It is described at five levels: atomic, molecular, cellular, tissue and whole-body.
  • Clinically the molecular and tissue levels are the most useful.

Compartment Models

  • 2-compartment model: body mass = fat mass (FM) + fat-free mass (FFM).
  • It assumes FFM density (~1.1 g/mL) and hydration (~73%) are constant.
  • 3-compartment model: FM + total body water + residual mass.
  • 4-compartment model: FM + water + bone mineral + residual.
  • The 4C model is the criterion (reference) method.

Fat Distribution

  • Subcutaneous fat (SAT) lies beneath the skin and is less harmful.
  • Visceral fat (VAT) surrounds abdominal organs and is more dangerous.
  • VAT drains into the portal circulation and releases pro-inflammatory adipokines.
  • Android ("apple") obesity is more risky than gynoid ("pear") obesity.

Assessment Methods

  • BMI and skinfolds: cheap but cannot separate fat from muscle.
  • BIA: a small current estimates total body water and FFM.
  • Hydrodensitometry and BOD POD measure body volume.
  • DXA: separates bone, lean and fat; the clinical reference.
  • CT/MRI are references for visceral fat and muscle; ultrasound and 3D scanning are newer and cheaper.
  • In physiotherapy, skinfolds and BIA give quick field estimates at the bedside; DXA is used when precision matters.

Validity and Reliability

  • Validity = agreement with a reference method.
  • Reliability = repeatability of repeated measurements.
  • BIA is useful for groups but not for individual diagnosis.
  • Use DXA or imaging when accuracy is important.

Norms and Change with Training and Age

  • Healthy body fat is roughly 10–20% (men) and 18–28% (women).
  • Sarcopenia = low muscle mass; sarcopenic obesity = low muscle plus high fat.
  • Resistance training raises FFM (about 1.1 kg in ~20 weeks).
  • Ageing shifts fat to visceral and intramuscular depots.

Clinical Relevance in Physiotherapy

  • Helps plan resistance training and protein intake in sarcopenic obesity.
  • Predicts weakness and delayed recovery after surgery or ICU stay.
  • Complements strength and gait assessment in older adults.
  • Used as an outcome measure in rehabilitation.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Guides resistance training prescription and protein advice in sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity.
  • Used in assessment of older adults to flag weakness, falls risk and slow recovery.
  • Monitors rehabilitation outcomes after surgery, ICU stay or prolonged immobilisation.

Body composition describes the fat and lean tissue of the body and is a better index of health than BMI. The physiotherapist uses it to guide training, interpret weakness and monitor recovery.

Recent advances

  • The ESPEN–EASO consensus gave the first algorithm for sarcopenic obesity.
  • DXA now reports visceral fat along with lean mass.
  • BIA phase angle is used as a clinical marker on its own.
  • Artificial intelligence is applied to CT/MRI for automated profiling.

References

  • Heymsfield SB, Lohman TG, Wang Z, Going SB. Human Body Composition. 2nd ed. Human Kinetics, 2005.
  • Ward LC. BIA for body composition assessment. Eur J Clin Nutr, 2019.
  • Donini LM, et al. ESPEN–EASO consensus on sarcopenic obesity. Clin Nutr, 2022.
  • Cruz-Jentoft AJ, et al. EWGSOP2 sarcopenia consensus. Age Ageing, 2019.

Q11 Influence of Age and Gender in Exercise and Training

People respond to exercise differently, and two key sources of this variation are age and sex. Physiological capacity and the response to training differ across the lifespan and between men and women. This affects how exercise should be prescribed. This answer describes sex differences, ageing changes and training responses at different ages and in both sexes.

Physiological differences between the sexes

  • Women generally have higher body fat and lower lean mass.
  • Men have greater absolute strength and muscle mass.
  • Men have a larger heart, greater blood volume and higher haemoglobin.
  • Men have a higher absolute V̇O₂max, but it narrows per kg lean mass.

Hormones and the menstrual cycle

  • Oestrogen supports muscle and promotes fat oxidation.
  • Progesterone has anti-oestrogenic effects.
  • Testosterone increases muscle protein synthesis and strength.
  • The menstrual cycle causes small changes in force and metabolism.
  • Cycle disturbances such as RED-S are clinically important.

Thermoregulation

  • Women start sweating at a lower core temperature.
  • Women have a lower maximal sweat rate and lose less fluid.
  • Men have greater sweating capacity and a larger surface area.

Ageing and aerobic capacity

  • V̇O₂max falls by about 5 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ per decade.
  • Aerobic capacity at 65 years is 30–40% lower than in youth.
  • Causes include lower maximal heart rate, stroke volume and oxygen extraction.
  • About half the decline is due to ageing and half to disuse.

Sarcopenia and other age changes

  • Sarcopenia is age-related loss of muscle mass, strength and function.
  • It involves impaired mTOR signalling, inflammation and loss of motor units.
  • Fast type II fibres and muscle power are lost most.
  • Bone mineral density falls, especially after menopause.
  • Loss of power and increased falls risk guide balance and power training in geriatric physiotherapy.

Training responses in older adults and both sexes

  • The capacity to adapt to training is preserved with age.
  • Older adults gain about 10–20% in V̇O₂max with aerobic training.
  • Resistance training increases mass, strength and power even in the very old.
  • Men and women adapt in similar ways; differences are mostly of magnitude.

Exercise prescription and children

  • Older adults need a multicomponent programme (aerobic, resistance, balance, flexibility).
  • Power training is valuable because power declines more than strength.
  • Children should not be treated as miniature adults.
  • Youth strength gains are mainly neural before puberty.
  • Supervised youth resistance training is safe and does not harm growth.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Guides individualised prescription: multicomponent programmes for older adults and cycle-aware training for female athletes.
  • Power and balance training reduce falls risk in geriatric rehabilitation.
  • Supervised youth resistance training is safe, which is relevant to paediatric physiotherapy.

Age and sex clearly influence exercise capacity and adaptation. Prescription must be individualised across the lifespan and between the sexes, but people of all ages and both sexes benefit from training.

Recent advances

  • Reviews show the menstrual cycle has only a trivial average effect on performance.
  • RED-S has replaced the female athlete triad as the preferred framework.
  • Consensus guidelines recommend multicomponent exercise for sarcopenia and frailty.

References

1. Hunter SK. The relevance of sex differences in performance fatigability. Med Sci Sports Exerc, 2016.

2. McNulty KL, et al. Effects of menstrual cycle phase on exercise performance. Sports Med, 2020.

3. Izquierdo M, et al. International exercise recommendations in older adults (ICFSR). J Nutr Health Aging, 2021.

4. Stricker PR, et al. Resistance training for children and adolescents. Pediatrics, 2020.

Q12 Electrical Properties of Muscle and Nerve

Nerve and skeletal muscle are excitable tissues. They can produce and carry electrical signals across their membranes. This property underlies movement, sensation and every form of electrotherapy. This answer explains the resting membrane potential, the action potential, conduction, the neuromuscular junction and the measures of excitability.

Resting Membrane Potential and Ionic Basis

  • RMP is the voltage across an unstimulated cell membrane.
  • The inside is negative: about -70 mV in nerve, -90 mV in muscle.
  • The membrane is far more permeable to K+ than to Na+ at rest.
  • Na+/K+-ATPase pumps 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in; it is electrogenic.
  • Inside is rich in K+; outside is rich in Na+ and Cl-.

The Action Potential

  • Reaches threshold (~-55 mV), so voltage-gated Na+ channels open.
  • Depolarisation: Na+ enters and the potential rises to about +30 mV.
  • Repolarisation: Na+ channels inactivate and K+ leaves the cell.
  • Afterhyperpolarisation: K+ channels close slowly.
  • Refractory periods: absolute (no new AP) and relative (stronger stimulus needed).

The All-or-None Law

  • Once threshold is reached, the AP is full size.
  • A stronger stimulus recruits more fibres, not bigger APs.
  • Spatial summation = more fibres; temporal = higher frequency.
  • Raising current in electrotherapy recruits more motor units.

Conduction in Nerve

  • Unmyelinated fibres use continuous conduction (slow, 0.5–2 m/s).
  • Myelinated fibres use saltatory conduction (fast, node to node).
  • Velocity rises with diameter and myelination.
  • Fibres are classed as A, B and C.
  • In physiotherapy, slowed conduction in carpal tunnel syndrome or Guillain–Barré explains weakness and sensory loss.

Neuromuscular Junction

  • The AP opens Ca2+ channels; Ca2+ triggers acetylcholine (ACh) release.
  • ACh binds nicotinic receptors and produces an end-plate potential.
  • A large safety factor means one nerve impulse gives one muscle impulse.
  • Acetylcholinesterase ends the signal.
  • This junction is the target in myasthenia gravis and curare.

Measures of Excitability

  • Rheobase = minimum current for a very long duration.
  • Chronaxie = pulse duration at twice the rheobase.
  • The strength–duration curve is hyperbolic.
  • Accommodation = a slowly rising current raises threshold.

Relevance to Practice

  • Current excites motor axons, not muscle fibres directly.
  • Pulses of about 100–300 µs excite sensory and motor nerves.
  • Frequency decides twitch or tetanus; amplitude decides recruitment.
  • EMG and NCS measure the M wave and conduction velocity.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Underpins electrotherapy dosing — rheobase, chronaxie and pulse width set the stimulus.
  • Explains assessment findings in EMG and nerve conduction studies.
  • Guides patient education on fatigue and recovery in myasthenia gravis and nerve injury.

Nerve and muscle work on ionic gradients and the all-or-none action potential. These principles directly guide electrotherapy, EMG and nerve conduction studies.

Recent advances

  • Cryo-electron microscopy clarified sodium and potassium channel structure.
  • Computer models now predict current spread and the site of spike initiation.
  • Kilohertz-frequency currents are more comfortable than low-frequency pulses.
  • Threshold tracking measures ion-channel function in vivo.

References

  • Guyton AC, Hall JE. Textbook of Medical Physiology. 14th ed. Elsevier, 2021.
  • Kandel ER, Koester JD, Mack SH, Siegelbaum SA. Principles of Neural Science. 6th ed. McGraw Hill, 2021.
  • Robertson V, Ward A, Low J, Reed A. Electrotherapy Explained. 4th ed. Elsevier, 2006.
  • Mogyoros I, Kiernan MC, Burke D. Strength–duration properties of human peripheral nerve. Brain, 1996.

Q13 Learning Models for Competency and Instrumentation

Physiotherapy education must produce competent graduates, not just knowledgeable ones. Educators use several learning models to explain how competence develops. Instrumentation means the tools used to measure whether competence has been achieved. This answer describes the main learning models and the assessment tools that operationalise them.

Behaviourist Model

  • Learning is a change in observable behaviour.
  • Uses stimulus, response and reinforcement.
  • The teacher breaks skills into small steps.
  • Good for manual skills, goniometry and patient handling.
  • Reinforced by checklist-based practical exams and drills.
  • Limitation: ignores reasoning, so it makes technicians.

Cognitivist Model

  • Focuses on mental processes: attention, memory and problem-solving.
  • The learner is an active processor of information.
  • Uses concept maps, worked examples and case discussion.
  • Cognitive load matters; teach complex tasks gradually.
  • Helps students transfer basic science into clinical problems.
  • Explains clinical reasoning and transfer of knowledge.

Constructivist Model

  • Learners actively construct knowledge from experience.
  • Based on the work of Piaget and Vygotsky.
  • The teacher is a facilitator, not a transmitter of facts.
  • Uses reflective journals, portfolios and problem-based learning.
  • Supports self-directed, lifelong learning.

Social Learning (Bandura)

  • Learning occurs by observation and modelling.
  • Four processes: attention, retention, reproduction, motivation.
  • Self-efficacy is the belief in one's own ability.
  • Uses role modelling, mentoring and supervised practice.
  • Supports cognitive apprenticeship in the clinic.

Experiential Learning (Kolb)

  • Learning is the transformation of experience.
  • Four-stage cycle: concrete experience -> reflective observation -> abstract conceptualisation -> active experimentation.
  • Experience alone is not enough; reflection is essential.
  • Learning styles: diverging, assimilating, converging, accommodating.
  • Fits clinical placements and simulation well.

Situated Learning and Reflection (Schön)

  • Learning is embedded in real activity and context (Lave and Wenger).
  • Learners move into a community of practice.
  • Schön described reflection-in-action and reflection-on-action.
  • Placements, debriefing and reflective writing are essential.
  • Reflection is an explicit domain in World Physiotherapy's framework.

Miller's Pyramid and Instrumentation

  • Four levels: knows, knows how, shows how, does.
  • Written exams test knowing; OSCE and simulation test "shows how".
  • Workplace-based assessment tests the apex, "does".
  • Tools must be valid, reliable, feasible and acceptable.
  • Rubrics, portfolios, OSCE, simulation and mini-CEX.
  • No single instrument measures every level of the pyramid.

Comparison with Later Models

  • Taba (1962): inductive, grass-roots, begins with needs.
  • Stenhouse (1975): process model; the teacher as researcher.
  • Outcome-based education: a modern descendant; "product defines process".
  • In physiotherapy, outcome-based education aligns with competency standards and accreditation.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Guides teaching of manual skills and patient handling through stepwise, reinforced practice.
  • Frames assessment via OSCEs, portfolios and workplace-based tools like mini-CEX.
  • Supports education of students for reflective, lifelong physiotherapy practice.

No single model explains competence. Miller's pyramid links the models to the instruments. A good curriculum aligns models, competencies and assessment.

Recent advances

  • Simulation and digital learning improve clinical reasoning.
  • Simulation-based assessment can identify threshold competence.
  • OSCEs show good reliability in musculoskeletal education.
  • Blended learning develops competencies like face-to-face teaching.

References

  • Bandura A. Social Learning Theory. Prentice Hall, 1977.
  • Kolb DA. Experiential Learning. Prentice Hall, 1984.
  • Schön DA. The Reflective Practitioner. Basic Books, 1983.
  • Miller GE. The assessment of clinical skills/competence/performance. Acad Med, 1990.

Q14 International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)

Earlier, disability was seen as a problem inside the person (medical model). Others said it comes from society and the environment (social model). The WHO combined both ideas into the ICF in 2001. This question asks about the ICF — its background, parts, codes and uses.

Background and Purpose

  • Adopted by the WHO in 2001.
  • Replaced the older ICIDH (1980).
  • Gives a common language for functioning and disability.
  • Used across disciplines, settings and countries.
  • Part of the WHO family with the ICD (diseases) and ICHI (interventions).

The Biopsychosocial Model

  • Functioning and disability come from a dynamic interaction.
  • The interaction is between the health condition and contextual factors.
  • So disability is neither purely body nor purely social.

Components of the ICF

  • Part 1: Functioning and Disability
  • Body functions and structures – impairments.
  • Activities and participation – limitations and restrictions.
  • Part 2: Contextual Factors
  • Environmental factors – facilitators or barriers.
  • Personal factors – age, gender, coping (not classified).

Qualifiers and Codes

  • Codes: b (body), s (structure), d (activity/participation), e (environment).
  • Example: b280 pain, d450 walking, e580 health services.
  • Qualifier scale: 0 none, 1 mild, 2 moderate, 3 severe, 4 complete.
  • Capacity is what a person can do; performance is what they actually do.
  • A "+" sign shows a facilitator (e.g. e310+2).

Interaction Model

  • The components affect each other in both directions.
  • Health condition→body functions→activities→participation.
  • Contextual factors act on all of them.
  • Example: a bad workplace lowers participation, which lowers fitness.
  • In physiotherapy, this explains why treating the impairment alone may not restore participation.

Difference from ICIDH

  • ICIDH was linear: impairment→disability→handicap.
  • ICF is interactive, not linear.
  • ICF uses neutral, positive words (functioning, activity, participation).
  • ICF added environmental factors as a full part.
  • ICF applies to all people, not only those with disability.

Applications in Physiotherapy

  • Assessment – covers impairment, activity, participation and environment.
  • Goal-setting – focuses on what matters to the patient.
  • Intervention – can target body, activity, participation or environment.
  • Outcome measurement – for example WHODAS 2.0.

Core Sets, ICF-CY and Rehab Cycle

  • Core Sets – short lists of key categories for a condition (e.g. stroke, low back pain).
  • ICF-CY – children and youth version (2007), now merged into the ICF.
  • Rehabilitation cycle – assessment, assignment, intervention and evaluation.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Assessment – structures the physiotherapy examination across impairment, activity, participation and environment.
  • Treatment planning – goal-setting targets the patient's real activities and participation, not just the impairment.
  • Outcome measurement – tools such as WHODAS 2.0 track functioning-based outcomes.

The ICF is the international standard for describing functioning and disability. It uses a biopsychosocial model and a common language. It helps physiotherapists plan patient-centred, functioning-based care.

Recent advances

  • The web-based ICF (2020) merges the former ICF-CY.
  • ICD-11 now includes a functioning assessment section.
  • Digital and electronic ICF tools are being used in practice.
  • Growing use of AI and machine learning in functioning assessment.

2. World Health Organization. ICF: Children and Youth Version (ICF-CY). Geneva: WHO, 2007.

Q15 Neuromuscular Electrical Stimulation (NMES) and Its Effects on Body Systems

NMES uses an electrical current to produce an involuntary muscle contraction. It works by depolarising the motor axons that supply a muscle. It is useful when a patient cannot or will not contract a muscle voluntarily. This answer covers its principle, parameters, effects on body systems, clinical uses and precautions.

Principle and Physics

  • Current passes through surface electrodes over the muscle or motor point.
  • It depolarises intramuscular motor axons.
  • Action potentials reach the neuromuscular junction and release ACh.
  • The evoked contraction is physiologically like a voluntary one.
  • Biphasic currents are preferred for comfort and tissue safety.

Stimulation Parameters

  • Frequency: 1–10 Hz twitch, 50–100 Hz tetanic.
  • Pulse duration: about 200–400 µs.
  • Amplitude: set to the maximum comfortable level.
  • Duty cycle: 1:2 to 1:5 to limit fatigue.
  • Ramp: 1–3 s for a smooth, comfortable contraction.

Mechanism of Activation

  • NMES recruits motor units in a non-physiological order.
  • Voluntary effort follows Henneman's size principle (small to large).
  • NMES recruits larger, faster, fatigable units first.
  • Fibres fire synchronously and near the electrodes.
  • This causes early fatigue, so generous rest is needed.

Effects on Skeletal Muscle

  • Increases strength and can cause hypertrophy.
  • With matched volume, gains equal conventional strength training.
  • Best evidence: quadriceps after knee surgery.
  • Helps in spinal cord injury (mass, torque and bone).
  • Less useful when voluntary contraction is possible.

Cardiovascular, Metabolic and Circulatory Effects

  • Acts as local exercise with a modest cardiac load.
  • Raises heart rate, cardiac output and blood pressure.
  • Increases local blood flow, oxygen use and glucose uptake.
  • The muscle pump raises venous velocity and volume flow.
  • Reduces oedema and may help prevent DVT.

Pain, Bone and Other Systems

  • Reduces pain through gate control (Aβ afferents).
  • Less effective than TENS for pure analgesia.
  • Loading by contraction can help maintain bone density.
  • Used in dysphagia (suprahyoid muscles).

Clinical Applications and Precautions

  • Early quadriceps training after ACL repair and knee replacement.
  • Spinal cord injury (often with FES cycling), ICU weakness and COPD.
  • Absolute: pacemaker/ICD, pregnant uterus, carotid sinus, active neoplasm, DVT.
  • Precautions: epilepsy, poor skin, obesity and cardiac disease.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • A core electrotherapy modality for early quadriceps activation after ACL repair or knee replacement.
  • Used in assessment of failure of voluntary activation (e.g. quadriceps arthrogenic inhibition).
  • Applied in ICU weakness, spinal cord injury and dysphagia; educate patients on fatigue and skin checks.

NMES produces an involuntary contraction with reversed recruitment and early fatigue. It is best used when voluntary activation is impaired, with an adequate dose.

Recent advances

  • Belt-electrode (B-SES) systems give broader, comfortable recruitment.
  • Superimposed NMES (NMES+) may outperform either modality alone.
  • NMES plus FES cycling preserves muscle and bone in spinal cord injury.
  • Central and reflex contributions to evoked force are now recognised.

References

  • Cameron MH. Physical Agents in Rehabilitation. 5th ed. Elsevier, 2018.
  • Maffiuletti NA, et al. Electrical stimulation for testing and training. Eur J Appl Physiol, 2011.
  • Stevens-Lapsley JE, et al. Early NMES after total knee arthroplasty. Phys Ther, 2012.
  • Jones S, et al. NMES for muscle weakness in advanced disease. Cochrane, 2016.

Q16 Methods of Data Collection

Data collection is the systematic gathering of information to answer a research question. Good data decides the value of a study. Physiotherapy uses many methods to measure pain, movement and function. This answer describes the main methods of data collection and how to choose among them.

Primary versus Secondary Data

  • Primary data are collected fresh for the study.
  • Secondary data already exist (records, registries).
  • Primary gives control; secondary is cheaper and faster.
  • Secondary data quality is often uncertain.

Observation

  • Records behaviour as it happens.
  • Can be participant or non-participant.
  • Structured observation uses a checklist.
  • Risks: reactivity and observer bias.
  • Captures movement during physiotherapy sessions, not attitudes.

Interviews

  • A verbal exchange with a participant.
  • Structured — fixed questions for everyone.
  • Semi-structured — topic guide with probing.
  • Unstructured — the participant leads.
  • Risks: interviewer bias, social desirability.

Questionnaires and Surveys

  • Written or electronic sets of questions.
  • Closed items are easy to code; open items are richer.
  • Items should be short and clear.
  • Must be valid and reliable.
  • Always pilot before full use.

Focus Groups

  • A moderated discussion of 6-10 people.
  • Group interaction reveals shared views.
  • Good for barriers and facilitators.
  • Risks: dominant voices, groupthink.

Scales and Measurement

  • Nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio levels.
  • The level decides which statistics to use.
  • PROMs like the Oswestry Disability Index are common.
  • Check responsiveness and MCID.

Records, Physiological and Digital Methods

  • Records review is efficient and historical.
  • EMG, spirometry, goniometry, dynamometry give precise data.
  • Digital tools and wearables give real-world data.
  • EMA reduces recall bias.
  • Risks: cost, privacy, adherence.

Choosing the Right Method

  • The choice is driven by the research question.
  • Consider quantitative, qualitative or mixed needs.
  • Think of cost, time and available expertise.
  • Respect ethical and privacy rules.
  • Triangulation combines methods for strength.

Advantages and Disadvantages

  • Questionnaires reach large samples cheaply.
  • Interviews give depth but take time.
  • Objective measures are precise but costly.
  • Every method is a trade-off between them.

Sources of Error

  • Measurement error — random or systematic.
  • Respondent error — recall and social desirability.
  • Researcher error — leading questions, poor coding.
  • Non-response bias — dropouts differ from joiners.

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Guides the choice of outcome measures (NPRS, ODI, goniometry) in physiotherapy research and audit.
  • Trains clinicians to collect reliable, valid data during assessment and follow-up.
  • Supports quality improvement by tracking pain, movement and function across a caseload.

Each method has its own strengths and errors. The right choice depends on the research question, and triangulation strengthens the results.

Recent advances

  • mHealth and wearables allow continuous remote data.
  • ePROMs and registries are now routine.
  • AI is being used to analyse sensor data.

References

3. Streiner DL, Norman GR. Health Measurement Scales. 5th ed. Oxford; 2015.

Q17 Tyler's Principles of Curriculum Development

Ralph Tyler (1949) is called the father of curriculum development. His book *Basic Principles of Curriculum and Instruction* gave a simple, rational way to plan a curriculum. His approach is known as the objectives or rationale model. This answer states his four questions and explains the model and its evaluation.

Ralph Tyler and the 1949 Book

  • Tyler published his basic principles in 1949.
  • It grew from the Eight-Year Study and his teaching work.
  • It offers "a rationale for viewing and interpreting the curriculum".
  • It is a theory of procedure, not of content.
  • It remains the dominant curriculum logic today.

The Four Questions and the Objectives Model

  • What educational purposes should be attained? (objectives)
  • What experiences can attain these purposes? (selection)
  • How can these experiences be organised? (organisation)
  • How can we determine whether purposes are attained? (evaluation)
  • The model is linear and means–ends; objectives are stated as changes in student behaviour.

Sources and Screens of Objectives

  • Three sources: learners, contemporary life, subject specialists.
  • Two screens: philosophy of education and psychology of learning.
  • The screens filter the many possible objectives.
  • Provides built-in needs analysis and stakeholder input.
  • Surviving objectives become the instructional objectives.

Selection and Organisation of Experiences

  • A learning experience = the learner interacting with the environment.
  • Experiences should allow practice and give satisfaction.
  • Continuity = repeated practice over time.
  • Sequence = deepening and broadening.
  • Integration = linking experiences horizontally.

Evaluation

  • Evaluation must be tied to the objectives.
  • Use multiple methods, not one test.
  • Instruments: observation, interviews, questionnaires and tests.
  • Evaluation is for curriculum improvement, not just grading.
  • In physiotherapy, evaluation includes OSCEs and clinical placement performance tied to the objectives.

Strengths and Limitations

  • Clarity, simplicity and alignment of all parts.
  • Accountability to students, institutions and society.
  • Linear and mechanical; can become rigid.
  • Behavioural objectives can trivialise learning; it is technocratic.
  • Flexible, because it may begin with any question.

Comparison with Later Models

  • Taba (1962): inductive, grass-roots, begins with needs.
  • Stenhouse (1975): process model; the teacher as researcher.
  • Outcome-based education: a modern descendant; "product defines process".

Physiotherapy relevance

  • Shapes physiotherapy curriculum design, from entry-level degrees to postgraduate courses.
  • Objectives and evaluation align with competency standards for clinical practice.
  • Supports assessment planning (OSCEs, clinical placements) tied to clear outcomes.

Tyler's four questions still guide curriculum planning. His model is clear and accountable but linear. Later models refined it without replacing it.

Recent advances

  • Recent work "In Defense of the Tyler Rationale" (2024) supports it.
  • A 2017 historical study shows it was a practical planning process.
  • It lives on in outcome- and competency-based curricula.
  • Constructive alignment and backward design keep its logic.

References
